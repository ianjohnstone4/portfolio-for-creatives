Overview

The problem was not chores. It was ambiguity.

SharedStandard was designed for young adult roommates living in shared housing, where conflict around chores often comes from unspoken expectations rather than a lack of care. In shared spaces like kitchens and bathrooms, people regularly assume they are on the same page, but their standards for what counts as done can be very different.

The concept helps roommates create a shared definition of done, pair it with a timing rule, confirm agreement together, and reduce the need for repeated reminders.

Why it mattered

Small mismatches repeated until they became social tension

Research showed that unclear expectations turn practical issues like dishes, counters, or bathroom cleanup into recurring interpersonal tension. In many shared homes, expectations stay implied rather than written down, and once one person feels forced to remind another more than once, the interaction quickly starts to feel like nagging.

“If I send one reminder, that should be enough, and beyond that it feels like nagging.”
Research

My research into shared-space expectations

Our research narrowed from broader roommate coordination to the different expectations people brought to shared spaces.

My research contribution

  • I conducted 5 of the team’s 7 interview sessions.
  • I completed 2 contextual inquiries, one on-site and one remote, alongside contextual observations.

Team research scope

Across the team, we conducted 6 individual interview sessions and 1 two-person session.

These counts refer to sessions, not unique participants.

What our research suggested

  • Completion meant different things. People could finish a chore and still disagree about the result. Dishwasher loading was a specific example.
  • Reminders could feel personal. Repeated follow-ups created social discomfort.
  • Existing routines mattered. Some households used chat or a whiteboard successfully; other calendars went unchecked.
Opportunity

Choosing a shared definition of done

I chose the Shared Standard Reset concept to focus on expectation alignment. The other concepts explored contribution visibility and routine cues. I made a shared definition of done the centre of the interaction so roommates could discuss the expected result before agreeing to a task.

My design guide

A consistent language for shared tasks

I created the design guide to give our screens a consistent reference. I used a neutral base, green actions, and Inter to support a calm tone, and defined spacing, action hierarchy, status labels, and input feedback.

Colour

Give actions a clear emphasis

Green marks primary actions, while white and pale grey separate content. Warning, information, and error states have their own colours.

Components

Make status visible

Buttons, status chips, and form feedback distinguish an available action, a pending agreement, and an error that needs attention.

Layout

Keep the structure predictable

The guide specifies Inter, an 8-point spacing system, 12-pixel card corners, and a 48-pixel minimum tap target.

Solution

A spaces first tool built around alignment, timing, and light touch accountability

The final concept was a mobile first shared household tool organized by spaces. Rather than using points, surveillance, or gamification, it focused on making expectations explicit and helping roommates coordinate with less friction.

Define the standard

Roommates choose a space and chore, then define what done means through a short checklist.

Set a timing rule

The app pairs each standard with a timing expectation so the routine is clear, not assumed.

Keep reminders gentle

A shared agreement and one gentle reminder are intended to reduce repeated, emotionally loaded follow-up.

Testing

Testing clarified what needed refinement

The team’s evaluative summary documents 4 moderated think-aloud tests. I completed 2 follow-up tests after the initial round. Those follow-up notes are not currently available, so I am not claiming a measured improvement from those sessions.

What testing revealed

  • The idea felt relevant and useful
  • The word “standard” felt abstract without support
  • Users wanted templates instead of blank input fields
  • Edit and update actions needed to be easier to find
  • The flow had to stay short and low effort

Priorities from testing

  • Keep examples and suggested checklist items prominent
  • Make the relationship between a chore, its criteria, and timing explicit
  • Give editing a clear, predictable entry point
  • Keep completion brief and test the value of each added step
  • Check whether roommates understand the pending agreement state
Outcome

SharedStandard reframed chores as a communication design problem

The outcome was a prototype built around clear expectations and mutual agreement. These principles guided the design; their effect on everyday household conflict has not been measured.

  • One shared definition of done

    A short checklist gives everyone the same reference point.

    Clarity

  • One gentle reminder

    A single prompt keeps follow-up brief and predictable.

    Restraint

  • No points, rankings, or surveillance

    The concept centres on agreement between roommates.
