The brief and the direction

Help visitors see
more possibilities.

The course brief asked us to rethink Granville Island’s website as a place for discovery and planning. The challenge was to help people explore beyond familiar attractions while keeping practical visitor information easy to reach.

We were designing for first-time visitors and people who already knew the island. Someone looking for a new experience needs a different starting point from someone checking a particular vendor’s hours.

Discover something

Surface current events and offer ways to browse food, arts and activities.

Decide whether it fits

Show the details that help someone judge a place or experience before committing.

Prepare for a visit

Connect discovery to hours, location, directions and planning support.

The early strategy work included personas, journey maps and a companion-app proposal. The journeys considered discovery, planning, preparation, visiting and returning, connecting an online choice to the information someone might need on the island.

These were planning tools developed from supplied course material, rather than additional interview participants. They helped frame structured planning and more spontaneous exploration.

This was an academic concept using an imagined client brief. The finished deliverable was a desktop Figma prototype, with broader mobile, map and itinerary ideas explored in wireframes.

My independent contribution

Give the content
a usable structure.

I completed the information hierarchy independently. I worked from a supplied historical content inventory whose URLs were inactive, using page titles and descriptions to infer content types and relationships.

The structural problem was broader than the menu. Recurring content needed a consistent model, and visitor tasks needed a clearer place alongside tenant, media and administrative resources.

Three structural decisions

Keep leaf content inside a useful parent
I grouped individual recipes beneath a recipe index and treated the “Top Ten” pages as content within a guide. Both decisions kept individual entries from becoming competing navigation choices.
Separate visitor and specialist tasks
I moved leasing, permits, tenant resources and media materials to secondary access points. They remained available without carrying the same navigation weight as planning, events or finding a vendor.
Make shared utilities global
I proposed search and an English/French toggle as site-wide elements. This offered direct access to utilities and avoided duplicating the navigation for language content.

These were proposed structural decisions. The IA was inferred from the supplied inventory and was not validated through a separate card sort or tree test.

The content model behind the pages

Define what each
entry needs to contain.

I separated recurring content into types such as events, vendor listings, recipes and listing indexes. Specifying their fields was intended to make information more consistent for visitors and easier to maintain.

Exploring the structure on screen

Make room for
different kinds of visits.

I created wireframes and worked with the team on the layout. The working file includes planning, directory, vendor, About and map explorations, along with early mobile layouts.

The planning direction broke a broad request, “plan my visit,” into interests, available time and accessibility preferences. Those inputs made the intended planning logic visible in the wireframe.

The map exploration paired a search and filter area with a results list and a map region. The goal was to connect discovering a place with understanding where it was. The map content remained a placeholder, and itinerary generation, saving and routing were not demonstrated as complete functions.

What we tested and what it changed

Follow how visitors
look for a day out.

I conducted three of the team’s five user-testing interviews. We used think-aloud, scenario-based tasks across the homepage, events, recommendations and vendor details, then recorded observations in a shared workbook.

The tasks followed a realistic sequence: find something to do this weekend, read an event’s details, explore quick food options, then find information about a specific vendor.

3 of 5

team interviews
conducted by me

The sample included people familiar with Granville Island and one person who had never visited. None had previously used its website to plan a visit. This was a small prototype study, with notes rather than a measured comparison against the existing site.

What the notes showedOur response and its limits

Finding a route

People used different entry points.

Some used the top navigation to reach events. Two tried the filter control before finding category browsing. A single route would not explain how everyone expected to move through the site.

Support browsing and direct access

The final concept offers top navigation, category cards and contextual links. Categories such as Quick Bites and Rainy Day Plans provide starting points around the kind of visit someone has in mind. Filter controls are visible concepts; their complete behaviour remains to be developed.

Workbook: interview rows 19, 23, 32 and 36–37.

Choosing a vendor

Finding hours did not answer every question.

Participants could locate hours and contact details. Requests for a menu showed that a person could still lack the information needed to decide where to eat.

Keep practical details visible and add menu access

We kept hours and location on recommendations, before opening a vendor profile, and added a View Menu action to the vendor page. The action is visible in the final design, while its destination remains incomplete in the sampled prototype.

Workbook: interview rows 26–31. Final vendor design: 377:566.

Knowing whether a place fits

Some needs required more specific content.

One participant wanted clearer dietary information. Another wanted to filter reviews. These requests exposed gaps beyond the basic address and opening hours.

Separate a visible revision from an unresolved need

The final vendor layout includes a review count and filter control. Dietary information still needs a clearer treatment. The displayed ratings and reviews are sample prototype content, and review filtering was not verified as a complete interaction.

Workbook: interview rows 30–31. Final presentation: pages 24 and 39.

A smaller iteration

Move weekly events ahead of the shortcut cards.

The colourful shortcut cards attracted early attention, so the team placed weekly events above them in the final homepage. This was a content-priority adjustment. The exact tested frame is not confirmed, and the revised order was not re-tested.

A closer look at the decision

Give someone enough
to choose a place.

The recommendation page shows hours and location before a visitor opens the profile. The vendor page then brings the name, hours, location and menu action together near the top.

This separates two jobs: scanning possible places and investigating one in more detail. It also responds to the menu request without making every recommendation card carry all of a vendor’s content.

The next question to test

Can someone with a dietary requirement decide whether this vendor is suitable, including reaching a complete menu? That task still needs a finished destination and another round of testing.

Connecting the screens

Carry the island’s
character through the UI.

The team’s visual direction used bright colour, photography and activity-led language to present Granville Island as a varied, welcoming place. Repeated headers, blue headings, cards and icon treatments connected the pages.

The icon set distinguished entertainment, location, visitor and activity types. Labels carried the meaning alongside the colour. The study also showed a trade-off: strong colour could draw attention away from the content we wanted visitors to notice first.

What we delivered and what comes next

A connected concept.
A clearer next test.

The project brought together content structure, wireframes, a five-session study and a shared desktop concept. My contribution was the independent IA, three interviews, wireframe work and collaboration on the layout.

Looking back, I would distinguish content findability, prototype completeness and usability outcomes more explicitly. A person finding a printed location does not establish that a map works, and a favourable reaction does not establish a change in real visits.

Delivered
Content types and fields, an independent hierarchy proposal, early desktop and mobile wireframes, research records, and the shared final desktop screens.
Explored
Map search, planning inputs, itinerary ideas and mobile layouts. These extend the direction, with important interactions and final states still unresolved.
Next validation
Finish one vendor-selection task, including menu and dietary information, then test it alongside event discovery on desktop and a matched mobile layout.

No post-revision study, production launch or measured impact on visits or vendor engagement is verified.
